## 1. Why this distinction matters

This is the most important conceptual distinction in the project.
Conflating the following notions leads to systematically incorrect sensitivity
estimates.

## 2. Three distinct notions

### 2A. Measuring a parameter or displacement at sub-Planck resolution

An instrument that resolves displacements `δx < l_P` is demonstrating
quantum-mechanical or technological sensitivity. It is **not** automatically
probing physical processes in which the Planck length appears as a relevant
scale.

### 2B. Probing a physical process in which a Planck-scale quantity appears

A physical process is Planck-sensitive if a Planck-scale quantity (l_P, t_P, m_P,
E_P) appears in its governing equation through a *physically meaningful* parameter,
not merely as a unit.

### 2C. Probing quantum gravity specifically

This is the strongest claim. It requires:

1. A physical effect predicted **differently** by quantum gravity vs. classical GR.
2. An experiment whose sensitivity is sufficient to distinguish these predictions.
3. A null result that falsifies a specific quantum-gravity model.

## 3. Quantum-mechanical sensitivity vs. gravitational sensitivity

An instrument may achieve quantum-limited sensitivity without being sensitive to
gravity at all (e.g., an optical clock probing atomic physics).

An instrument may be sensitive to gravitational effects without probing Planck-scale
physics (e.g., a gravimeter measuring g).

Both distinctions must be made explicit for every candidate experiment.

---

## 4. Required analysis for every approach

For each approach in `approaches/`, the following questions must be answered
explicitly:

1. What physical quantity is being measured?
2. Does a Planck-scale parameter appear in the governing equation?
3. If yes: in what combination and with what suppression factor?
4. Is the sensitivity quantum-mechanical, gravitational, or both?
5. What is the ratio of the predicted Planck-related signal to the best demonstrated
   experimental uncertainty?
